# The lineage we are building for

## Barcelona, 1901

On 8 September 1901 Francisco Ferrer i Guàrdia opened the Escola Moderna on Barcelona's Carrer de les Corts with thirty students; it reached 126 by 1905 [1]. What made it dangerous was its refusal of the apparatus: no exams, no prizes, no punishments, no grades, on the grounds that these teach children to perform rather than to think [1][2]. Tuition slid with what parents could pay, classes went out to factories and parks, and boys and girls sat together — in Catholic Spain, itself the argument.

The part usually forgotten is that the school was also a press, publishing forty-odd of its own textbooks plus a monthly *Boletín* [1] — and the press is why the model travelled. By late 1905 there were fourteen Ferrer-style schools in Barcelona and thirty-four across Catalonia, Valencia and Andalusia, plus roughly 120 rationalist schools run by republicans and freethinkers using his materials [1]. **The artifact propagated the school; the institution did not.**

Authorities closed the school in 1906 over its association with Mateu Morral's attempt on Alfonso XIII; Ferrer spent a year in jail and was acquitted for lack of proof [1]. In 1909, after Barcelona's Tragic Week, he was arrested for a rising he did not mastermind and shot at Montjuïc on 13 October — Paul Avrich called it "judicial murder" [1]. His last words: "Aim well, my friends. You are not responsible. I am innocent. Long live the Modern School!" [1] The execution did what the school could not: Ferrer schools appeared in Geneva, Liverpool, Milan, São Paulo and New York [1][3].

## Stelton, 1915

American anarchists founded the Ferrer Association in 1910 and a Modern School at the Ferrer Center in 1911 — Goldman and Berkman among the founders, Will Durant as principal, Robert Henri teaching art — on borrowed premises, moving from St. Mark's Place to 12th Street to 107th Street [3][4]. In 1915, after men connected to the Association tried to bomb John D. Rockefeller, the community left the city, bought sixty-eight acres in Piscataway, New Jersey, and built the Stelton colony around the school [4][5]. Mohegan followed in Westchester in 1923 [6]; Modern Schools in Chicago, Los Angeles, Salt Lake City and Seattle each lasted a few years [3].

Stelton lasted from 1915 to 1953, and the reason is a warning. Elizabeth and Alexis Ferm — "Aunty" and "Uncle" — arrived in 1920 and rebuilt it around crafts, printing and play; the children hand-set and printed their own paper from 1922 [4]. What ended it was attrition, not pedagogy: the Depression, the splits over Spain and Stalin, Camp Kilmer next door, Aunty Ferm's death in 1944, Uncle Ferm carrying it alone to the end [4]. **Two people held a school together for thirty years, and when they were gone it stopped** — the continuity problem in its purest form.

## Deschooling, 1960–1972

The 1960s revival ran on books. A. S. Neill's *Summerhill* (school founded 1921, American edition 1960) made non-compulsory attendance thinkable to a mass audience [7]. Paul Goodman, the best-known anarchist in the US after *Growing Up Absurd* (1960), wrote in *Compulsory Mis-education* (1964) that "the compulsory system has become a universal trap, and it is no good," and his alternatives read like a product spec: "use the city itself as the school — its streets, cafeterias, stores, movies, museums, parks and factories," and "use appropriate unlicensed adults of the community — the druggist, the storekeeper, and the mechanic — as the proper educators of the young" [8]. Toronto's free skoolers would later teach on sidewalks, in laundromats and on picket lines, in that lineage [9].

Then Ivan Illich. Chapter VI of *Deschooling Society* (1971) [11], "Learning Webs," is the direct technical ancestor of a needs board. Illich proposed four networks in place of school: reference services to educational objects; **skill exchanges**, "which permit persons to list their skills, the conditions under which they are willing to serve as models for others"; **peer-matching**, "a communications network which permits persons to describe the learning activity in which they wish to engage, in the hope of finding a partner for the inquiry"; and reference services to educators-at-large [10]. The peer-matcher is specified almost as pseudocode: the user "describe[s] the activity for which he sought a peer. A computer would send him back the names and addresses of all those who had inserted the same description" [10]. Illich also named the failure mode to design against: "Skill teachers are made scarce by the belief in the value of licenses. Certification constitutes a form of market manipulation and is plausible only to a schooled mind" [10].

In practice the movement ran on a newsletter. The New Schools Exchange, founded at the March 1969 Conference on New Schools in Menlo Park and published from Santa Barbara from 21 April 1969, was "the primary connector for the free schools movement": it kept "a current published list of all known free schools," matched teachers and families to schools, and cost "$1 per month… or whatever you can afford" [12]. A directory, a classifieds page and a calendar — the only real infrastructure the movement had, and the place its cause of death is recorded. Alicia Richard's study of the NSE archive finds three recurring causes: "a lack of secure access to short or long-term funding sources; difficulties in finding a building that they could securely settle into; and the interpersonal problems that arose after the initial excitement of starting a school wore off" [12]. The Portland Free School's 1969 self-report is the canonical venue story: "We opened without a building and for several weeks we 'floated', meeting in parks… We soon moved out again when the building was found not to satisfy city codes" — then Reed College lent its Student Union for a year, and "The S.U. will not be available this year, so we are again without quarters" [12]. On founders: "long hours, low or no pay, philosophical disagreements with parents — all the ingredients for quick burn-out" [12]. Kozol's *Free Schools* (1972) supplied the critique from inside: a romantic free school for privileged white families amounted to "a sandbox for the children of the SS Guards at Auschwitz" — a line he later regretted, but which forced the movement to say who it was for [13][14].

## The skool spelling, 1999–

The modern wave is smaller and more honest about its scale. The Anarchist Free Space and Free Skool began in April 1999 when a free skool at Toronto's closing Community Cafe moved into a Kensington Market storefront [9]. Its organizing artifact was a **course calendar**, carrying the vision on its front page: "Education is a political act… sharing skills and exchanging experiences in an egalitarian, non-hierarchical setting free of prejudice, we challenge disempowering habits" [9]. The course list was deliberately not all anarchism — "Love Songs of the 20s and 30s," "Street Art," yoga, a Situationist night walk called The Drift — and for most of a year at least one class ran every weekday evening [9]. Facilitators, not instructors, took responsibility "to photocopy and make readings available, and ensuring that the space was available and open and people welcomed" [9]; anyone broadly agreeing with non-authoritarian principles could join the collective [9].

Jeff Shantz's chapter is also the best post-mortem we have, and it is unsentimental. The lending library "suffered regular neglect as no one seemed interested in taking care of it. Eventually it fell into complete disrepair" [9]. Consensus was "in many ways unsuited to a lively activist group," and meetings "bogged down in hours of heated discussion over whether activist posters could be placed in the windows" [9]. And the line worth laminating: "One must be careful not to underestimate the rather large amounts of real labor required to keep a TAZ running… As a sign in the Free Space proclaims, 'Anarchy doesn't mean dirty dishes.'" [9] It did not die so much as metabolise: it "morphed to become the Anarchist University" [9].

Free Skool Santa Cruz, begun spring 2004, is the model most worth copying, because it survived by being small and owning nothing. "The skool is decentralized — there is no main campus or office. Classes are held in public spaces, reserved community areas or private residences" [16]. Anyone can apply to teach [15]. Money is out: "We try to keep Free Skool firmly fixed in a gift economy and outside the stream of commerce. While some teachers may ask for a donation to cover materials, no one is turned away for lack of funds" [17]. The project "strives to blur the lines between teacher, learner, and organizer" [17]. Its core artifact is a quarterly paper calendar left in restaurants and public spaces — and its own archive of the 2005–2014 run is, literally, a folder of those calendars [15][19]. It ran year-round to 2014, went quiet, was revived by SubRosa collective members in 2021 as a seasonal project, and is running a 2026 session [17]: dormancy and revival, not a clean death. Its organizers named the real enemy in 2011 — "Free Skool has experienced a lack of continuity" from "lack of dedication, organizers coming and going, and those involved changing every season," and an earlier incarnation simply "petered out" [15].

The most useful contemporary document is the Neighborhood Anarchist Collective's *Grow Your Own Free Skool* (Eugene, August 2025), a how-to built from FreeSkool Eugene's successes and failures, published as a Creative Commons printable zine [18]. Its lineage is explicit — "inspired by the Escuela Moderna (Modern School) of the 1900s, Toronto's Anarchist Free University (AFU), and Free Skool Santa Cruz" — and its first principle is Lex's: "free skool is built on the belief that everyone is a teacher and everyone is a student" [18]. On venues it is unromantic: established venues "may not be free and will limit the type of classes that can be offered," while houses "usually accommodate fewer people, force the host to assume responsibility, and could potentially endanger someone's housing situation" [18]. On rhythm: "Some communities keep classes going year-round; others operate seasonally… Remember to be patient; growing something new takes time" [18]. Because "not everyone has access to or is on social media," it insists on "paper flyers in public spaces and through word of mouth" [18]. And on safety it is concrete where the 1970s were vague: ask about access needs in advance, avoid jargon, sit in a circle, "get enthusiastic consent before touching a participant" [18].

## Seven small skools, 1998–2026

Seven local projects, read from their own archived pages, say the same things (the full case files are appended below). **Freeskool Asheville** — Lex's lineage, and the name matters, since there was never an "Asheville Free School" — described itself in terms we could paste into a product brief: "There is no physical school, center, institution, or corporation involved. Teachers (like you) find space and put on free classes for community members (like you); this website provides the tools to create classes and get the word out," with classes visible "on this website and on **printed calendars** in cetain local hot-spots around Asheville" [20, sic]. It ran 2009–2013 and left no closing statement, because its memory was a volunteer's rented domain and then a Facebook group, and both are gone [20][22].

**Free Skool Olympia** published a monthly PDF calendar with "paper copies in envelopes posted around downtown," proposed next month's classes at a potluck, and refused "to delineate between roles of 'student' and 'teacher'" [23][24]. In April 2011, losing momentum, it asked the city a needs-board question: "Is there something you'd like to learn?" [24] **Portland Free Skool** — "decentralized… it exists wherever & whenever someone wants to teach something for free" — advertised a single unpaid "Creative/Outreach Director" in May 2012, filled it in June, and never posted again [27]. **Ithaca Freeskool** ran a decade and ended in pure silence, its last post a cheerful note about a $300 fundraiser [25][26]. **Baltimore Free School** claimed "Anyone can teach a course… propose one right now!" while in practice running an approval queue, lost its classroom when Red Emma's moved in 2018, went quiet for eight years, and is running again in 2026 [28][29][30][31].

**Toronto** supplies the verdict twice. Its 1998 school published a calendar "every four months" and existed because one member "generously provided us with a rent-free space" [32]; its successor, Anarchist U, ran on "$50 a year" with "no single street address" [33][34]. Then it lost the room. Kalin Stacey, in Megan Kinch's 2013 post-mortem: "that's something the anarchist free school that happened in the late '90s in Kensington had that made it really effective. And, **when they lost the space, they lost the school**" [35]. The AFU "eventually became homeless" to gentrification, and "without a geographical space or a strong activist movement, the AFU stagnated" [35]. Kinch also records the person-dependency — one quiet figure who kept it running, who died in 2012 — and the one structural answer anyone in this lineage found: Hamilton Freeskool's "four committed organizers serving **8-month overlapping terms**" [35].

Only **the Purple Thistle Centre** (Vancouver, c.2000–30 April 2015) ended on purpose, and it wrote it down: "Refusing to Jump the Shark, OR: the Thistle was never meant to be an institution." It is honest that the cause was both choice and money — "hard funding cuts," "the signs are lining up (low participation, funding issues, slowing of momentum)" — and it refused the institutional rescue because that "would ultimately undermine the core of what the Thistle was/is: a space run by youth" [36][37]. Two lines belong on the wall: "**we wish more places would end when it's time — but unfortunately we live in a system that values longevity over thriving; and that aint us**," and "**Not one person *is* the Thistle**" [37].

## What the pattern says

Five things recur across 125 years. **The calendar or zine is the school** — Ferrer's *Boletín*, Stelton's student-printed paper, the New Schools Exchange newsletter, Toronto's course booklet, Santa Cruz's quarterly calendar, Eugene's zine. **Anyone can teach** is the rule nobody compromises, from Goodman's druggist to Illich's skill exchange to "everyone is a teacher and everyone is a student." **Money is the boundary of the thing**: the free skools that refused it held, while the 1960s free *schools* that took tuition died of finance. **Space is the recurring crisis**, not interest or ideas — Portland floating in parks, the Ferrer Center moving three times, Santa Cruz answering by refusing to have a campus. And **horizontality is always partly fiction**: someone is always doing the photocopying and the dishes, and when that crew leaves — the Ferms, Santa Cruz's seasonal organizers — the project stops, unless the artifact can be picked up by strangers.

# Ten design constraints

**1. The calendar is the school.**
Every surviving free skool ran on a periodic printed calendar — Santa Cruz's quarterly zine, Olympia's monthly PDF with "paper copies in envelopes posted around downtown," Toronto's booklet published "every four months," Asheville's "printed calendars in cetain local hot-spots," the New Schools Exchange newsletter — and Santa Cruz's entire archive of its 2005–2014 life *is* a folder of those calendars, while Asheville, whose calendar lived only in a Joomla database, left nothing [9][12][15][19][20][24][32].
*Implication:* the app's primary output artifact is a print-ready monthly/seasonal calendar zine (imposed for folding, black-and-white, legible photocopied), generated in one click by anyone, with the web view as a secondary rendering of the same data.

**2. Needs board before class listings.**
Illich's learning web begins with skill exchanges and peer-matching — people posting what they can teach and what they want to learn — not with a schedule of offerings, and his peer-matcher is literally a board you post a description to [10]; Free Skool Olympia, losing momentum in 2011, reached for exactly this — "Is there something you'd like to learn?" [24] — and Freeskool Asheville's first meeting produced a 28-item community wish-list of classes nobody had yet offered [20].
*Implication:* the first screen is a two-column needs board ("I can share" / "I want to learn") that works with zero scheduled events, and scheduling a class is a secondary action taken on a match, not the entry point.

**3. No money, anywhere in the product.**
Santa Cruz kept itself "firmly fixed in a gift economy and outside the stream of commerce," while the 1960s free schools that depended on tuition and grants died overwhelmingly of "lack of secure access to… funding sources" [12][17], and the grant-funded Purple Thistle closed partly because of "hard funding cuts" [37]. Olympia went further than we need to: "free from compulsory tuition, dues, or fees of any sort" [23].
*Implication:* no payments, no tickets, no fees, no donate button, no Stripe, no "suggested price" field — the only money-adjacent affordance is an optional free-text supplies note on a single class.

**4. No ratings, no reviews, no credentials.**
"Skill teachers are made scarce by the belief in the value of licenses. Certification constitutes a form of market manipulation and is plausible only to a schooled mind" [10]; Ferrer abolished grades and prizes for the same reason [1], and Baltimore and Ithaca answer the same FAQ the same way — "What if I want to teach something, but I'm not really an 'expert'? No problem!" and "You don't have to be an expert!" [25][29].
*Implication:* no stars, no review text, no attendance counts displayed, no teacher badges, no verification checkmarks, no leaderboards, no "popular classes" — the only social proof is that a human being wrote the listing.

**5. Radically open membership, asserted not granted.**
"If you say you're part of Free School, you're part of Free School" is the operational form of "everyone is a teacher and everyone is a student" [18]; Toronto admitted anyone who broadly agreed with non-authoritarian principles [9], while Baltimore — which claimed "Anyone can teach a course… propose one right now!" — in practice ran "course proposals that we have approved," and Anarchist U admitted it suspended proposals falling "short of the community standards of quality" [30][34]. Both drifted into a queue; do not build one to drift into.
*Implication:* no application, no approval queue, no invite codes, no member directory with tiers; posting a class or a need is self-service and live immediately, with ATProto identity meaning anyone with a handle is already a member.

**6. Admin-free by default; moderation is a removable add-on.**
Toronto's consensus process "bogged down in hours of heated discussion over whether activist posters could be placed in the windows," and the project was damaged more by governance than by outsiders [9].
*Implication:* ship with no admin role at all — authors edit and delete their own posts and nothing else — and make any later moderation capability an optional, per-instance, clearly-labelled overlay rather than a structural assumption.

**7. Venue is a first-class object, and the app assumes no venue.**
Space is the movement's permanent crisis: Portland "floated, meeting in parks," the Ferrer Center moved three times, and Santa Cruz's answer was to have no campus — "there is no main campus or office" [12][16]. Anarchist U ran on "volunteer labour and donated spaces" for "$50 a year" [33], Toronto's 1998 school existed only because one member "generously provided us with a rent-free space" [32], and Baltimore lost eight years when Red Emma's moved out from under its classroom [28]. The verdict is Kalin Stacey's: "**when they lost the space, they lost the school**" [35]. Eugene documents the real tradeoffs between cafés, houses and parks, including that a house can "endanger someone's housing situation" [18].
*Implication:* venues are reusable records with capacity, accessibility, cost and host-risk notes, any class can be posted as "venue needed," and a class with no venue is a valid, visible, findable state — not a validation error.

**8. Failure-tolerant: an empty calendar is a normal state.**
"Sometimes nobody comes and that's part of it"; Eugene's advice is "Remember to be patient; growing something new takes time," Santa Cruz went dormant 2014–2021 and came back, Baltimore came back after eight years when a room reappeared [28][31], and Olympia could say publicly that it "hasn't been seeing very much participation in the last months" without that being the end [17][18][24]. The Purple Thistle names the anti-pattern exactly: "we live in a system that values longevity over thriving; and that aint us" [37].
*Implication:* no empty-state shaming, no engagement metrics, no streaks, no "your skool is inactive" nags, no automatic unpublishing; a cancelled or unattended class is recorded plainly and the calendar prints fine with three entries.

**9. Forkable: every skool is a copy someone else can run.**
Ferrer's movement spread because he published his textbooks, not because he franchised schools — 120 rationalist schools used his materials [1] — Eugene calls its guide "a living document" under Creative Commons [18], and Baltimore released its own site software as GPLv3 so other cities could "grab it or fork it on Github" [29]. Against that: Freeskool Asheville's record lived on one volunteer's rented domain and then a Facebook group, and both are gone [20][22].
*Implication:* data is portable by construction (ATProto records in the user's own PDS), the codebase and the zine templates are open-licensed, self-hosting a new city's skool is a documented first-class path, and nothing lives only in a central database we control.

**10. Continuity beyond founders, designed in.**
Stelton ran thirty years on two people and ended when they were gone [4]; Santa Cruz named its own cause of death — "organizers coming and going, and those involved changing every season" [15]; Toronto's library "fell into complete disrepair" because nobody owned it [9]. Portland advertised one unpaid "Creative/Outreach Director," filled it in June 2012, and never posted again [27]; Anarchist U lost the one person who quietly kept it running [35]. The Purple Thistle shows the good version — fifteen youth-run years, then a deliberate close, and "Not one person *is* the Thistle" [37] — while Hamilton Freeskool supplies the only structural answer anyone in this lineage found: "four committed organizers serving **8-month overlapping terms**" [35].
*Implication:* no single-owner objects — every skool, venue and recurring class has an append-only public history any participant can continue, no account can be the sole key to anything, and the app ships an explicit "hand this off" flow plus an always-current printable "how this skool works" page so a stranger can restart it after a year of silence.

# Appendix — the seven case files

**Freeskool Asheville** — Lex's own lineage, and the name matters: there was no "Asheville Free School." The project was *Freeskool Asheville*, and it is recoverable only because `freeskoolasheville.org` was a two-line frameset pointing at a volunteer's personal domain, `joshfox.com/freeskool/` [20]. (The plausible-looking `ashevillefreeschool.org` is a GoDaddy parking page in every capture — a dead end I followed first.) It was live by February 2009 and still being written about in January 2013 [20][21]. Its self-description is almost exactly our product brief: "Freeskool Asheville is an interactive, decentralized model for learning—without the limitations of hierarchy… We are all students and we are all teachers," and "A lot of inspiration to create Freeskool Asheville came from the Santa Cruz Freeskool" [20]. More precisely:

> "In a nutshell, Freeskool Asheville is a teaching and learning network by and for the community. **There is no physical school, center, institution, or corporation involved.** Teachers (like you) find space and put on free classes for community members (like you); this website provides the tools to create classes and get the word out." [20]

Teachers were told to "find a place for your event. Consider having it at your house, one of Asheville's many parks, a local coffeeshop, or in your neighbor's yard," and that "your class will be visible to the general public on this website and on **printed calendars** in cetain local hot-spots around Asheville" [20, sic]. Classes ran to "music theory instruction, herb walks in the woods, discussions on anarchism, hide-tanning, bird-house-making, book clubs, monkey chanting, physics for artists, and picnic get-togethers in the park" [20]. *Mountain Xpress* covered a "Freeskool community sing" in May 2011; *Shareable* called it the city's learning "hub" in January 2013 [21][22].

Nobody wrote a closing statement. What the record shows instead is a software death in three stages: a banner appears on every page — "Freeskool Asheville is now using a Facebook group to organize events. **It is a temporary solution until this website's bugs are fixed**" — then the calendar empties ("No events" by October 2013), then the domain lapses and serves spam by January 2015 [20]. The Facebook group it migrated to has no archive at all [22]. **No burnout, venue loss or funding crisis is attested, because the project's memory was a rented domain and a Facebook group, and both are gone.**

**Free Skool Olympia** (c. 2010–late 2011) wrote the best principles of the set — Autonomy, Community, Empowerment, Enjoyment, Solidarity, Totally Free — including "We refuse to delineate between roles of 'student' and 'teacher' as we all have something to learn and something to share," and a money rule broader than ours: "In addition to being free from compulsory tuition, dues, or fees of any sort, the Free Skool must always encourage the free exchange of speech, ideas, knowledge and understanding" [23]. It published a **monthly** PDF calendar — "Download it, print it out, frame it, give it to your friends, or flyer it around town," with "paper copies in envelopes posted around downtown" — and proposed the next month's classes at a potluck, the Free Skool Feast, at Media Island [24]. Its April 2011 post is the most useful sentence in this survey: "The Free Skool hasn't been seeing very much participation in the last months, so we'd like to ask all you folks out there in the community about you needs… Is there something you'd like to learn?" [24] A project with a working calendar reaching for a needs board. It cited Free Skool Santa Cruz's class-proposal form and the Black Panthers' "Black Child's Pledge" as sources [23]. No closing statement; about a year of calendars.

**Ithaca Freeskool** (2007–2017) is the longest-running of the US group and died of pure silence. Its rule: "We are all teachers! All ages, all skill levels welcome!… You don't have to be an expert!" [25] Its 2007–08 post titles are the whole operating model — "Teach for the Freeskool," "New calendars are out," "New outreach poster," "Summer class proposals deadline May 10th" [26]. Unlike Olympia it took small civic grant money, from the Sustainable Tompkins mini-grant program [25]. The final post, June 2017, is cheerful: a swap fundraiser raised $300. Then nothing, ever, with no announcement.

**Portland Free Skool** (2011 – June 2012) is the clearest burnout record. "Portland Free Skool is decentralized. It exists wherever & whenever someone wants to teach something for free!" [27] Venues were Laughing Horse Books, a Food Not Bombs serving in Colonel Sumner Park, private addresses, "RSVP for location" [27]. Paper calendars went out by volunteer — there is a post titled "Volunteers needed to distribute October calendar in their local neighborhoods" [27]. Then in May 2012 it advertised a single unpaid coordinator: "Want to make a community difference? Be a Creative/Outreach Director @FreeSkool!… Now accepting requests for this soul-rocking, **non-monetary** position." The role was filled in June 2012. The blog stops that month [27].

**Baltimore Free School** is the one that came back, and it came back when a room did. It launched from Red Emma's in 2009 needing "roughly $500/month… for rent," then "spun off as a separate project with a distinct collective, and with 501c3 status under the Fusion Partnerships umbrella," with bi-weekly open organizing meetings [28][29]. Its claim was maximal — "Anyone can teach a course or organize a discussion or workshop at the Free School; propose one right now!" and "Is the Free School really FREE? Yes! Promise!" — and its website software was released as GPLv3 for other cities to fork [29][30]. Courses run through the 2018 captures and vanish from the 2019 ones, matching Red Emma's departure from 30 W. North Avenue in 2018, which took the classroom with it [28]. Red Emma's own history calls it "a bit of a hiatus" [28]. It is running again in 2026 at 3128 Greenmount Avenue [31]. **Venue loss caused an eight-year gap; the idea survived it.**

**Toronto, twice.** The 1998–99 Free School published a calendar "every four months," took proposals at "monthly meetings," and existed because "the oldest anarchist in our group… generously provided us with a rent-free space from which to operate" [32]. Its successor, the Anarchist Free University, ran 2003/04–c.2010 on "$50 a year" [33], with "no single street address," courses of ten weeks, materials "under $30 per course," proposals by consensus at a monthly general meeting — and a candid FAQ entry: "**I signed up to a committee and nothing seems to be going on.** It's quite common for some of the committees to lie dormant until a new project gets proposed" [34]. Megan Kinch's 2013 post-mortem in *Fifth Estate* gives us the single best sentence in this entire lineage, from organizer Kalin Stacey: "that's something the anarchist free school that happened in the late '90s in Kensington had that made it really effective. And, **when they lost the space, they lost the school**" [35]. The AFU then "eventually became homeless" as "high rents meant collectives had trouble keeping infoshops or punk venues open," and "without a geographical space or a strong activist movement, the AFU stagnated" [35]. Kinch also records the person-dependency: one "central and persisting figure," Erik "Possum" Stewart, did "the low level maintenance, often in subtle ways helping consensus work or nudging things along, keeping spaces like the Anarchist 'U' running" — and died suddenly in 2012 [35]. And she records the one structural answer anyone in this lineage has found: Hamilton Freeskool ran as "a consensus-based collective of four committed organizers serving **8-month overlapping terms**" [35].

**The Purple Thistle Centre** (Vancouver, c.2000–30 April 2015) is the only project here that ended well, and it left a letter. Founded by Matt Hern "with seven teenage friends," it was "run by a youth collective that controlled all the day-day operations"; Hern's summary is "Sadly, it closed in 2015 after 15 years. Its time had come" [36]. (Hern dates the founding to 2001; the letter says "14 and half years" in January 2015, implying mid-2000.) The collective's own January 2015 letter is titled "Refusing to Jump the Shark, OR: the Thistle was never meant to be an institution," and is honest that the cause was both choice and money: "the Thistle has hit some hard funding cuts this past year… energy and funding at the Thistle has been dwindling"; "the signs are lining up (low participation, funding issues, slowing of momentum), it's time to be responsive to that and trust ourselves"; and the line to put on the wall — "**we wish more places would end when it's time — but unfortunately we live in a system that values longevity over thriving; and that aint us**" [37]. They explicitly refused the institutional escape: they could have kept the money by "partnering with a few institutions," but that "would ultimately undermine the core of what the Thistle was/is: a space run by youth" [37]. "To borrow on a Quakers ethos, sometimes you gotta just **lay it down**" [37]. And the continuity principle, stated better than any theorist manages it: "**Not one person *is* the Thistle**, so be sure to talk to folks who have been involved with the project, their individual or collective stories will be worth hearing!" [37]

# Confidence / not verified

**Directly verified from primary or near-primary text (high confidence).** Every quotation attributed to Ferrer's school, Goodman, Illich, Shantz, the New Schools Exchange (via Richard's thesis), Free Skool Santa Cruz and the Neighborhood Anarchist Collective was read in the source document and transcribed from it, not from a summary. The Shantz chapter was read as a full PDF scan of pages 124–144; *Compulsory Mis-education* and *Deschooling Society* were read as full texts; Richard's thesis was read as a full PDF; the NAC guide and the Santa Cruz about page were read as live HTML on 2026-09-12.

**Verified but from encyclopedic sources (medium-high).** Escuela Moderna enrollment figures, school counts, the Morral affair, the Tragic Week trial and the execution date come from Wikipedia's *Escuela Moderna* and *Francisco Ferrer* articles, which cite Avrich and Fidler. The Ferrer Center / Stelton / Mohegan timeline, the Ferms, and the 1953 closure come from the Alliance for Self-Directed Education's history plus Wikipedia; the Rutgers Modern School collection would be the archival source to check before publication.

**Secondhand (medium).** Kozol's "sandbox for the children of the SS Guards at Auschwitz" phrasing is quoted from the Sage *Encyclopedia of the Social and Cultural Foundations of Education* and from Richard's thesis, not from the 1972 book itself — the page reference in *Free Schools* is **not verified**, and the exact wording varies slightly between sources ("children of" vs "the children of"). The Portland Free School report is quoted from Richard's transcription of New Schools Exchange no. 24 (1 November 1969); the original issue was not consulted.

**Not verified.**
- **"Any space can be a classroom"** is widely associated with Free Skool Santa Cruz but I could **not** verify it as a literal quotation from their FAQ or calendars. The verifiable equivalent is "there is no main campus or office. Classes are held in public spaces, reserved community areas or private residences" [16]. Do not put the phrase in quotation marks in published copy.
- **The origin of the "skool" spelling.** The Toronto project used it from 1999 and the usage spread through the 2000s, but I found no source establishing who coined it or why. Treat the 1999 Toronto attribution as "earliest well-documented use," not "origin."
- **Founding year of Free Skool Santa Cruz.** *City on a Hill Press* (2011) says spring 2004; the project's own current about page says it "first existed year-round from 2005 – 2014." Both are in the essay; 2004 as founding and 2005 as the start of continuous year-round operation is the most likely reconciliation, but this is an inference.
- **Freeskool Asheville — a correction worth flagging.** My first pass concluded the trace was unrecoverable, because `ashevillefreeschool.org` is a GoDaddy parking page in every capture. That was wrong and the name was wrong: the project was **Freeskool Asheville**, its domain was `freeskoolasheville.org`, and that domain was only a frameset — the content lived at `joshfox.com/freeskool/`, which is why nothing surfaces by searching. All quotations in the regional section come from those archived pages [20]. Verified active February 2009 – January 2013; **founding date not verified** (no founding statement survives; the calendar's year dropdown starts at 2010). Only two independent, non-self-published mentions exist: one *Mountain Xpress* listing (May 2011) and one *Shareable* article (January 2013) [21][22]. **No cause of closure is attested anywhere** — the visible sequence is website bugs → migration to a Facebook group → empty calendar → lapsed domain. Burnout, venue loss and funding are all plausible and none are documented, so the essay says only what the record supports. **Lex's own account is the primary source and this document must not be used to correct it.**
- **Exact closure dates** for Free Skool Olympia, Ithaca Freeskool and Portland Free Skool are absent from the public record: none of the three published a closing statement, and the dates in this essay are "last activity observed" (Olympia late 2011, Portland June 2012, Ithaca June 2017), not announced endings. **Baltimore is the exception that corrects an assumption in the brief:** it did not wind down — it lost its classroom when Red Emma's left 30 W. North Avenue in 2018, went quiet for roughly eight years, and is running again in 2026 at 3128 Greenmount Avenue [28][31]. The venue-loss attribution is an inference from the capture record aligning with Red Emma's move plus Red Emma's own phrase "a bit of a hiatus"; no one states it outright.
- **The Purple Thistle's founding year is contested by its own sources:** Matt Hern says 2001 [36]; the closing letter says "14 and half years" in January 2015, implying mid-2000 [37]. The closure is both a deliberate choice and a funding story — the letter says both, and it would be a misreading to quote only the "Its time had come" half.
- **Toronto's founding date has two versions.** *Fifth Estate* #353 says the school "got up and running in October, 1998" after an August 1998 proposal [32]; Shantz dates the Anarchist Free Space and Free Skool to April 1999 [9]. These are most likely the free skool and then the Kensington storefront, but that reconciliation is my inference. Similarly, the AFU's founding is "Fall 2003" by its own course archive and "the summer of 2004" per the *Globe and Mail* [33][34] — I cite both rather than choose.
- **Anarchist U's end date is not verified.** No closing statement exists; the last documented course term is Spring 2010 and the last wiki edits are March 2012 [34]. The domain now serves unrelated content.
- **Ithaca Freeskool took grant money** (Sustainable Tompkins mini-grants) [25], unlike the strictly-autonomous Olympia model — relevant if anyone argues the no-money rule is universal in practice. It isn't; it is universal in *stated principle*.
- **No Boulder or Front Range free-skool precedent found.** Searches for a Boulder or Denver free skool in the 2000s returned nothing citable. If one existed, it left no web trace I could find — which is itself an argument for constraint 9 (forkability) and constraint 1 (a printable artifact that outlives a website).

**A note on method.** The regional survey (sources 20–37) was produced by a dedicated archival research pass working the Wayback CDX API and direct archive fetches, because almost none of these sites are indexed by live search. I independently re-verified the two most load-bearing documents myself: Megan Kinch's *Fifth Estate* #388 article (the "when they lost the space" quote) and the full text of the Purple Thistle closing letter. The remaining archived pages in 20–31 were retrieved once each; every URL is given so they can be re-checked, and archive.org rate-limits aggressively, so expect 429s on a fast re-run.

**A note on length.** The essay proper runs ~2,450 words against a requested 1,200–1,600; the detailed case files are in the appendix rather than the body to keep the essay closer to two pages. The brief asked for nine distinct historical threads, each carried by citable quotation; after two compression passes, further cuts meant dropping either a thread or the evidence for it, and I judged the evidence more valuable than the word count. **If a hard two-page limit is needed, cut in this order:** (1) the "Seven small skools" section down to Asheville, Toronto and Purple Thistle (the appendix holds the rest), (2) the Barcelona execution paragraph to two sentences, (3) the Ferrer Center / Stelton paragraph to two sentences, (4) the NAC paragraph's rhythm and channels quotes. What must survive, because the constraints rest on it: Illich's four networks, the three New Schools Exchange obstacles, Shantz's post-mortem, Santa Cruz's money and continuity statements, Olympia's monthly calendar, and the NAC's "everyone is a teacher and everyone is a student."
